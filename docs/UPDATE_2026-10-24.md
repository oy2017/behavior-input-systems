# Update, 2026-10-24: why demographics fail, and choosing traits per survey

Two follow-up experiments to the main study ([README](../README.md)), both on Gemini 3.5 Flash with
reasoning off, same people and same hidden questions as before.

## Headline findings

1. **One prompt line of base rates closes most of the demographics gap, but the cause is not settled.** Telling
   the model how people with the same gender, age band, and education answered each question raised
   accuracy on all nine survey datasets (by 1 to 11 points) to within 0.5 to 5 points of the statistical
   yardstick. Population answer shares alone, with no profile, beat the demographic profile on eight of nine
   (mean +3.3), so most of the gain does not depend on the person's group. The design cannot separate the
   model not knowing the rates from not using what it knows.
2. **The borrowed trait set hurt the type vector, but the method's gain is not yet established (see the placebo pilot and corrections below).** With traits chosen per
   questionnaire by Jackson et al.'s search, nerdiness goes from 29% to 38 to 40%, level with the person's
   full answer history. Openness, missing from the borrowed trio, was the trait that mattered.
4. **The base-rate fix does not transfer to new questions.** Rates on the five most similar *other*
   questions recover only about a quarter of the same-question gain (mean +1.5 vs +5.1 points), and a
   single questionnaire-wide group average does nothing or hurts. As a method it is a lookup for
   questions that have been asked before, not a way to answer new ones.
3. **But a three-number type has a ceiling.** On vocational interests chosen traits lift it from 30% to
   35 to 37%, still 14 points below history; neither Big Five nor Holland's own categories summarise 48
   specific activity ratings. Behavior history remains the best description on every dataset but one.

## 1. Is the demographics failure a knowledge problem? Give the model the base rates.

**Question.** In the main study the model given a demographic profile scored below "guess each
question's most common answer," while a statistical model given the same profile gained 1 to 5 points
over that baseline. One explanation is that the model cannot reason from a profile; another is that it
simply does not know how people in each group actually answer. The statistical model learns those
group answer rates from training data. So we told the model the rates.

**Two new conditions.** `baserate`: the prompt states how all other respondents answered this question
("across all other respondents the answers were distributed as 1: 20%, 2: 20%, ..."). `demographics +
baserate`: the profile plus how respondents with the same gender, age band, and education answered
(falls back to the population rates when the group has under 15 people). Rates come from 3,000 to 6,000
other respondents in the same raw file, never from the people being tested.

**Result** (accuracy, %; yardstick = the statistical model given demographics):

| Dataset | nothing | demographics | population base rates | demographics + group base rates | yardstick | history |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Big Five items | 29.3 | 29.0 | 32.1 | 34.1 | 35.6 | 47.1 |
| Machiavellianism | 33.2 | 28.5 | 37.4 | 39.2 | 42.3 | 44.2 |
| Depression-anxiety-stress | 25.5 | 30.1 | 32.5 | 35.2 | 35.7 | 52.9 |
| Nerdiness | 34.4 | 27.3 | 38.1 | 36.7 | 41.3 | 37.7 |
| Vocational interests | 22.5 | 27.5 | 29.6 | 31.4 | 36.7 | 49.7 |
| Temperament | 40.7 | 42.2 | 43.0 | 46.2 | 47.5 | 55.7 |
| Nonverbal behavior | 27.0 | 34.3 | 35.2 | 35.0 | 36.5 | 52.2 |
| General Social Survey | 48.3 | 47.9 | 49.3 | 50.5 | 52.3 | 56.1 |
| World Values Survey | 34.7 | 41.8 | 41.6 | 46.4 | 47.3 | 53.2 |

**Reading.**
- Group base rates lift the demographics condition on every dataset, by 0.7 (nonverbal behavior) to 10.7
  (Machiavellianism) points, and bring it to within 0.5 to 5.3 points of the yardstick, which stays ahead on all
  nine. This shows that supplying the rates helps. It does not show why: the model may not know the
  rates, may know them and not use them when answering as a person, or may simply follow the most common
  answer once it is printed in the prompt. Separating these needs a further test, such as prompting with
  the model's own estimated rates.
- Population base rates alone, with no profile at all, beat the demographic profile on eight of nine
  datasets (mean +3.3; World Values Survey is the exception at -0.2). Adding the group-specific rates and
  the profile adds a further 1.8 points on average, higher than population rates on seven of nine. Most of the gain
  is therefore about the question, not the person. With about 1,000 records per dataset, gaps of one or
  two points are within sampling noise; we have not yet computed confidence intervals.
- Base rates do not substitute for behavior history: history is 1 to 18 points above demographics plus
  group rates on all nine datasets. Nerdiness is the near-tie (37.7 against 36.7, and 38.1 with population
  rates alone).
- Spread (W) barely moves with base rates on most datasets (it improves on distress, from 34.5 to 23.9),
  so the gain is in placing individuals, not in reproducing the population.

**Implication for prompting.** If a demographic profile is all that is available, the cheapest fix is to
add the group's answer distribution for each question from any existing survey of the same population.
This recovers most of the yardstick with no model training.

## 2. Choosing the type-vector traits per survey

The main study used one Big Five trio (Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism), borrowed from the
trio Jackson et al. found best for economic games, on every survey. Here we run their selection
procedure per survey on the two datasets where that default did worst, with three candidate pools, and
with the scoring rule aligned to the paper (mean distance to all draws rather than to the median).

- Nerdiness: the five Big Five traits (25 subsets of one to three), and five traits the model proposed for
  this questionnaire (intellectual curiosity, social introversion, sci-fi and fantasy affinity,
  self-perceived unconventionality, academic orientation).
- Vocational interests: the five Big Five traits, and Holland's six interest types (41 subsets).

Selection is on half the people (even record index), reporting on the other half, so the chosen subset
is never scored on the data that chose it. Tables used 3 levels and 3 draws per type and item.

### Nerdiness (NPAS): accuracy on the held-out half, %

| Pool | best single trait | best pair | best trio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Big Five | Openness, 37.0 | Openness + Neuroticism, 39.0 | Openness + Agreeableness + Neuroticism, 38.2 |
| model-proposed | self-perceived unconventionality, 37.0 | sci-fi affinity + academic orientation, 35.6 | intellectual curiosity + social introversion + self-perceived unconventionality, 40.2 |

Reference points on the same half: the borrowed trio (Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism) scores
29.2 under the same rule; nothing 34.4; demographics 27.3; measured TIPI 33.0; history 37.7.

**Reading.** Choosing the traits for the questionnaire turns the type vector from the worst description
on this dataset into the best: +9 to +11 points over the borrowed trio, level with or above the person's
full answer history (37.7). Openness is the trait that matters, as the topic suggests, and it was not in
the borrowed trio. The model-proposed pool did as well as the Big Five with a trio and better with a
single trait that names the construct directly. One trait already recovers most of the gain; the
second and third add 1 to 3 points.

### Vocational interests (RIASEC): accuracy on the held-out half, %

| Pool | best single trait | best pair | best trio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Big Five | Openness, 30.2 | Openness + Extraversion, 37.0 | Openness + Conscientiousness + Neuroticism, 36.2 |
| Holland's six interest types | Artistic, 27.8 | Artistic + Social, 35.4 | Realistic + Artistic + Social, 33.0 |

Reference points on the same half: the borrowed trio 30.4 under the same rule; nothing 22.0;
demographics 27.6; measured TIPI 26.0; history 50.6.

**Reading.** Choosing traits helps here too (+5 to +7 over the borrowed trio) but far less than on
nerdiness, and the type vector stays 14 points under history. Two things stand out. First, Holland's
own six categories, the field's theory of vocational interests, did no better than the Big Five; a
three-level rating of "Artistic" is a coarse summary of 48 specific job activities. Second, selection
is noisy at this sample size: the trio chosen on the selection half was not the best trio on the
reporting half (36.2 chosen vs 38.6 best; 33.0 vs 39.0 for Holland), with gaps of 3 to 6 points, so the
honest held-out numbers are the chosen ones and the search needs more than 500 people per half to be
reliable.

### What the two pilots say together

1. Superseded by the larger rescore below. This item originally said a chosen type vector matches or beats
   full history on nerdiness; history itself is near the most common answer there, so that is not evidence of skill.
2. Superseded by the larger rescore below. This item originally said the type vector works on dispositions and not
   on specific tastes. The rescore shows the opposite pattern on these two datasets, so that claim is withdrawn.
3. Model-proposed traits are a viable pool: on nerdiness they matched the Big Five with a trio and gave
   the best single trait. They cost one extra call and need no theory.
4. The search is affordable per survey (30k to 100k calls here at 3 levels and 3 draws) but noisy with
   1,000 people; use larger samples or fewer, theory-driven candidates when scaling it to all datasets.

## 3. Does the base-rate fix generalize to questions nobody has asked? (added 2026-10-27)

**Method, stated as a recipe.** Base-rate-augmented persona prompting: before asking the model how
person *x* answers question *q*, (1) group the profile (here gender x age band x education, falling
back to the population when a group has under 15 people); (2) look up, in an existing survey of the same
population, how *x*'s group answered; (3) add one sentence with that distribution to the prompt; (4) ask
as usual. Section 1 used the group's rates on *q* itself, which requires that *q* has already been asked.
Here we test the two ways the rates could come from elsewhere:

- **related-question rates**: the group's answer distributions on the five other items of the same
  questionnaire most similar to *q* (TF-IDF cosine similarity on the item texts), with *q* excluded. This
  is the "new question in a known domain" setting.
- **questionnaire mean**: a single number, the group's average answer over all items of the
  questionnaire; only on the seven datasets whose items share one scale.

**Result** (accuracy, %; same people and questions as section 1):

| Dataset | demographics | + same-question rates (sec. 1) | + related-question rates | + questionnaire mean | history |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Big Five items | 29.0 | 34.1 | 30.3 | 29.7 | 47.1 |
| Machiavellianism | 28.5 | 39.2 | 32.2 | 21.4 | 44.2 |
| Depression-anxiety-stress | 30.1 | 35.2 | 33.2 | 30.5 | 52.9 |
| Nerdiness | 27.3 | 36.7 | 28.7 | 25.3 | 37.7 |
| Vocational interests | 27.5 | 31.4 | 28.4 | 26.1 | 49.7 |
| Temperament | 42.2 | 46.2 | 43.3 | 43.8 | 55.7 |
| Nonverbal behavior | 34.3 | 35.0 | 35.6 | 33.0 | 52.2 |
| General Social Survey | 47.9 | 50.5 | 47.7 | - | 56.1 |
| World Values Survey | 41.8 | 46.4 | 43.1 | - | 53.2 |
| mean gain over demographics | | +5.1 | +1.5 | -1.3 | |

**Reading.**
- Related-question rates help a little on eight of nine datasets (+0.9 to +3.7) and not at all on the
  GSS (-0.2), recovering on average about 30% of the same-question gain (range 15% to 61%; nonverbal behavior
  is the exception where the same-question gain was itself under one point). Knowing how a group
  answered neighbouring items tells the model something about the group's general stance, but not the
  item-specific rate that did the work in section 1.
- The single questionnaire-wide average hurts on four datasets (Machiavellianism by 7 points) and helps
  slightly on three (+0.4 to +1.6). One possible reason, not tested, is that a one-number summary misleads
  the model on items keyed in the opposite direction.
- So the one-line fix is a lookup, not a model: it works when the exact question has been asked of the
  same population before, which is also when a lookup table would do. Its value is diagnostic (supplying
  the rates recovers most of the gap) and as a floor for simulations of known questions; it is not a general
  method for new questions. Transfer would need a better way to carry group statistics across items,
  for example an item-level model of how the group's stance maps onto new wording, which is a different
  project.

## Correction, 2026-11-01: "below the most common answer" does not establish stereotyping

The main study compared the model's single draw with the most common answer and called the shortfall a
stereotype effect. A one-draw simulator with perfect knowledge of the population scores below the most common
answer by construction, so the comparison is not like for like. From existing outputs
(`scripts/suite/mode_vs_sampler.py`; "sampler" is the expected accuracy of one draw from the real distribution):

| Dataset | demographics | mode | sampler | demographics - mode (95% CI) | demographics - sampler (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| bigfive | 29.0 | 30.7 | 25.2 | -1.7 (-5.0, +1.6) | +3.8 (+1.3, +6.5) |
| mach | 28.5 | 38.6 | 27.1 | -10.1 (-14.1, -6.3) | +1.4 (-1.4, +4.1) |
| dass | 30.1 | 33.8 | 27.5 | -3.7 (-6.3, -1.1) | +2.6 (-0.0, +5.2) |
| npas | 27.3 | 40.1 | 28.1 | -12.8 (-16.9, -8.7) | -0.8 (-3.6, +1.9) |
| riasec | 27.5 | 31.5 | 23.9 | -4.0 (-8.0, -0.1) | +3.6 (+0.9, +6.3) |
| fti | 42.2 | 44.7 | 34.0 | -2.5 (-5.8, +0.8) | +8.2 (+5.4, +11.0) |
| nis | 34.3 | 35.7 | 27.5 | -1.4 (-4.3, +1.5) | +6.8 (+4.1, +9.4) |
| gss2022 | 47.3 | 50.6 | 40.1 | -3.3 (-6.5, -0.2) | +7.2 (+4.6, +9.7) |
| wvs7 | 41.2 | 42.8 | 31.1 | -1.6 (-4.7, +1.5) | +10.1 (+7.5, +12.6) |

The shortfall against the most common answer is clear on five of nine datasets, and the model beats the sampler on
eight of nine, clearly on six. The fair test, not yet run, is the most frequent answer over repeated draws. This
also affects the yardstick gaps, since the yardstick reports its most likely answer.

## Correction, 2026-11-01: reference points for the trait search

On the half of people used for reporting, nerdiness scores are: most common answer 41.0, population rates 39.0, history 38.4, nothing 35.8, chosen type vectors 38.2 to 40.2 (n = 500). The chosen traits beat the borrowed trio, but they do not beat the most common answer, so nerdiness shows no person-level gain yet. Vocational interests does: most common answer 31.6, population rates 30.4, chosen type vectors 36.2 to 37.0, history 50.6. Headline 2 above overstated the nerdiness case. No placebo traits have been run.

## Placebo pilot, 2026-11-01

Traits with no plausible link to either questionnaire (Jackson et al.'s five placebo traits) went through the same search on the same people. Scores are on the held-out half, n = 500. Intervals are paired bootstraps, uncorrected for multiple comparisons (`scripts/suite/placebo_compare.py`).

| Dataset | Traits | Placebo | Big Five | Other pool | Most common answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nerdiness | 1 | 27.0 | 37.0 | proposed 37.0 | 41.0 |
| Nerdiness | 2 | 31.0 | 39.0 | proposed 35.6 | 41.0 |
| Nerdiness | 3 | 36.4 | 38.2 | proposed 40.2 | 41.0 |
| Vocational interests | 1 | 25.4 | 30.2 | Holland 27.8 | 31.6 |
| Vocational interests | 2 | 28.2 | 37.0 | Holland 35.4 | 31.6 |
| Vocational interests | 3 | 29.2 | 36.2 | Holland 33.0 | 31.6 |

Using the set size picked on the selection half, which was three traits in every pool:

- Vocational interests: Big Five minus placebo +7.0 (1.8, 12.2); Holland minus placebo +3.8 (-1.6, 9.2).
- Nerdiness: Big Five minus placebo +1.8 (-2.4, 6.0); proposed minus placebo +3.8 (-0.8, 8.4).
- Against the most common answer, only the Big Five pair on vocational interests clears zero, +5.4 (0.4, 10.6), and it was not the pre-picked size. The Big Five trio just misses, +4.6 (-0.4, 9.8).

**Reading.** Trait choice beats placebo clearly on one dataset and not on the other, so the claim is alive but narrower than headline 2 stated. Placebo traits never clear the most common answer. On nerdiness every chosen placebo set includes "fondness for the smell of old books," which may relate to items such as "I collect books," so it may not be a true placebo there. Two datasets and 500 people per half are too few for a firm conclusion. Tables do not depend on the people scored, so scoring more people needs no new API calls.

## Larger rescore on 5,000 new people, 2026-11-01

The decision rule was committed before results were computed (`scripts/suite/placebo_rescore.py`): no new API calls,
5,000 people unused by the search, two-fold selection so everyone is scored out of sample, 8 Holm-corrected tests, and
support on a dataset only if a real pool beats both placebo traits and the most common answer. After the first run we
found the most common answer had been computed from about 50 answers per item, a noisy baseline that favored the type
vectors. We fixed it to use all answers from the other fold and reran; the rule did not change. The reproduction check on
pilot people gave 37.0, 29.0, and 39.0 against an expected 37.0, 29.2, and 39.0.

| Dataset | Pool | Accuracy | Minus placebo (95% CI over people) | Minus most common answer (95% CI over people) | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nerdiness | Placebo | 33.7 | | -6.6 (-8.5, -4.7) | |
| Nerdiness | Big Five | 37.8 | +4.1 (2.7, 5.5) | -2.5 (-4.4, -0.7) | Not supported |
| Nerdiness | Model-proposed | 37.3 | +3.6 (2.2, 5.0) | -3.0 (-4.8, -1.2) | Not supported |
| Vocational interests | Placebo | 29.6 | | -2.8 (-4.6, -1.1) | |
| Vocational interests | Big Five | 38.9 | +9.3 (7.6, 11.0) | +6.5 (4.8, 8.2) | Supported |
| Vocational interests | Holland | 36.1 | +6.5 (4.7, 8.3) | +3.7 (2.1, 5.2) | Supported |

Most common answer: 40.3 on nerdiness, 32.5 on vocational interests. Borrowed Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism
trio: 31.2 and 32.2. On a bootstrap over target items, which treats questions as a sample, the intervals are wider: all
four placebo gaps stay above zero, and vocational interests stays above the most common answer. The Big Five nerdiness
shortfall then includes zero (-5.4 to 0.2).

**Reading.** Chosen traits beat placebo traits on both datasets, placebo traits never reach the most common answer, and
the pilot's near-tie on nerdiness was noise. The full rule is met on vocational interests only. These people have no
LLM history runs, so the comparison with history (50.6 on the pilot half) is not like for like. Two datasets cannot show
when the method works, so the explanation in the withdrawn item above should not be replaced with another one yet.

## Scale-bug correction, 2026-11-01

The General Social Survey and World Values Survey type tables had the wrong answer scale on 38% and 43% of rows, now fixed. Earlier WVS and GSS figures also used only people with a fitted type: demographics on all 1,000 people is 41.2 on WVS and 47.3 on GSS, not the 41.8 and 47.9 in the tables above. The base-rate gains in those tables were computed on matching records, so the conclusions do not change.

## Changes to the main findings

- Finding 3 (the model's single draw does not beat the most common answer) has a partial explanation: supplying base rates for the exact question closes most of the
  gap, and supplying them for related questions closes about a quarter. Whether the model lacks the rates
  or fails to use them is still open.
- Finding 5 (a fitted type beats measured traits, trails history) needs the qualifier "with a default
  trait set": with traits chosen per questionnaire the type vector can match history on dispositional
  questionnaires. The main README has been updated accordingly.
