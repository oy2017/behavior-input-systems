# Paper plan: what survives when you describe a kind of person to a language model?

Written 2026-11-08, before the round-trip results existed. Decision rules are in the docstring of
`scripts/suite/roundtrip.py`, committed in the same change.

## The question

Every method that simulates people with a language model, from silicon samples to digital twins to synthetic users,
has one step in common: a description of a person goes in, behavior comes out. How faithful is that step? We measure
it with a round trip. Real groups of respondents are learned from their survey answers, so we know how each group
actually answers. Each group is then described to the model, the model answers as the described person, and we score
it against the group's own held-out members. Anything lost between the group and its description, or between the
description and the answers, shows up as a gap.

## Why it matters

- Persona prompts are usually written, not measured. Nobody checks whether the persona behaves like the people it
  stands for, because the people are not available. Here they are.
- If the step distorts in a known direction (for example, toward caricature), every downstream simulation inherits it,
  and some of it may be correctable.
- It tells practitioners what to feed the model: a short sketch, a full profile, numbers, or a distribution request.

## What we already know going in (from docs/TYPE_VECTOR_DIAGNOSTICS.md)

- 27 data-learned groups predict held-out answers better than the most common answer on all 8 questionnaires, and
  better than fitted type vectors on all 8. The types exist.
- Describing those groups in 80 words and handing the description back loses most of that: never better than type
  vectors, worse than the groups on 7 of 8.
- Exploratory: the model's answers for a described group are about twice as far from average as the group's own; the
  direction is right on 76 to 93 percent of items; the pattern correlates with the real group at 0.2 to 0.8. Damping
  recovers most of the loss on two questionnaires and hurts on others.

## What the paper adds

1. The round-trip test itself: a way to score any persona description against the behavior of the people it summarizes.
2. A richness curve: a 12-item sketch, a full portrait, an item-by-item verbal profile, a numeric profile. Each leaves
   out the item being predicted, so nothing can be copied; the model must generalize from who the person is.
3. The mechanism, confirmatory: exaggeration, direction, pattern, at individual and population level.
4. Three candidate fixes, with pre-committed success criteria: damping, a moderation instruction, and asking for the
   distribution instead of an answer. Reasoning as a robustness check.
5. Context from the type-vector study: trait words chosen per questionnaire beat placebo words, yet type vectors beat
   the most common answer on only 2 of 8 questionnaires.

## Possible readings, fixed in advance

- Richer descriptions recover the groups: the loss was summarization. Advice: give full profiles, not sketches.
- Richer descriptions do not recover the groups: the model cannot generalize from a complete profile of a kind of
  person to a new question; its picture of who answers what together is wrong. Advice: use data, not descriptions,
  wherever the question has been asked before.
- Either way, if a fix works on 6 of 8, the paper has a constructive half.

## Outline

1. Introduction: the description step; the round trip; contributions.
2. Related work: silicon samples and twins (Argyle; Park; Toubia's mega-study); personas miss covariance (Bisbee; Jia);
   caricature (Cheng; Wang); type vectors (Jackson); data-driven personas (Salminen and Jansen).
3. Method: data and groups; descriptions; scoring at individual and population level; pre-registration.
4. Results: richness curve; mechanism; fixes; reasoning; type vectors as context.
5. Discussion: what to feed a model; what the failure says about the model's folk psychology; limits.

## Limits to state

One model family (Gemini 3.5 Flash), three draws, 27 groups, English questionnaires from self-selected online samples
plus two national surveys, descriptions generated by the same model that answers them.
