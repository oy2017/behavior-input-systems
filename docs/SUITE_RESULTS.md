# Survey suite: the input-system comparison on nine more datasets (Gemini 3.5 Flash, 2026-10-18)

Tables: [suite_tables.md](suite_tables.md) (generated by `scripts/suite/aggregate_suite.py`).
Same design as [RESULTS.md](RESULTS.md): same model, same task prompt, same 1,000 subjects per dataset,
only the description of the subject varies; one draw per record; the target is one held-out item per subject.

Conditions: `none`, `demographics`, `traits` (measured: TIPI scores, political identity, or WVS value
indices), `history` (the subject's other answers), combinations, and a best-fitting type vector over
three Big Five traits (Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism on a 3-level scale: the trio Jackson et
al. found best for economic games by search, applied unchanged to every survey; fitted on the other answers only,
used either for type-based prediction or as the system prompt).

## Findings across ten datasets

1. **Behavior history wins everywhere.** The subject's own prior answers are the best single input
   on all ten datasets, by 3 to 27 points over nothing (3.3 on NPAS, 7.8 on GSS, 11 to 27 elsewhere).
   Adding demographics on top of history changes accuracy by between -2.3 and +1.7 points.
2. **Demographics are usually worth little, and on three datasets they hurt.** Gains over nothing:
   MACH -4.7, NPAS -7.1, GSS -0.4, SocSci210 +1.2, FTI +1.5, Big Five +1.7, DASS +4.6, RIASEC +5.0,
   NIS +7.3, WVS +7.1. The two clear positives are the datasets where demographics plausibly
   carry the target: NIS (gender and age for nonverbal behaviour) and WVS (country for values).
   On SocSci210, a sixteen-field profile with party and ideology adds one point on real experiments.
3. **Demographics can lower individual accuracy while improving the distribution.** On GSS, W per
   item falls from 23.9 to 15.5 with demographics and accuracy does not move: the model shifts its
   answers toward the right groups without placing individuals. On MACH and NPAS the profile
   pulls the model away from the population mode toward stereotypes and accuracy drops.
4. **Measured traits help only when the trait is the construct.** TIPI scores add 14.6 points on
   DASS (neuroticism is distress) and 11.6 on Big Five items (the same construct), but nothing on
   NIS, FTI, MACH, or NPAS. Political identity does not help on GSS; the WVS value indices add 5.
5. **A best-fitting type vector beats measured traits on six of eight datasets** where both exist (MACH +7.1,
   DASS +8.3, RIASEC +4.8, FTI +1.9, NIS +15.6, GSS +3.6), loses on NPAS and WVS, and loses to raw history
   everywhere (by 6 to 19 points). Fitting three trait levels to a person's own answers extracts
   more than their self-reported scores on the same traits, but a three-dimensional type vector is a lossy
   summary of a response record. Where the fitted axes are wrong for the domain (nerdiness needs
   openness and conscientiousness; values are not personality) it falls below the baseline.
6. **Distributional alignment follows the same ordering** (see suite_tables.md): history brings W
   to 6 to 16 on the 0-100 scale, demographics to 10 to 35, nothing to 12 to 47. The `none`
   condition collapses to a single answer on DASS (sd ratio 0.00) and to under half the human
   spread on RIASEC, FTI, NPAS.

## Against the supervised ceiling

Pooled HistGBT trained on 4,000 to 5,000 other respondents from the same raw file, with every item of every
training respondent as a target and the item id as a feature; evaluated on the same 1,000 subject-item pairs.
Accuracy, LLM / ceiling:

| Dataset | none | demographics | measured traits | history |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MACH | 33.2 / 38.6 | 28.5 / 42.3 | 30.9 / 42.0 | 44.2 / 52.8 |
| DASS | 25.5 / 33.8 | 30.1 / 35.7 | 40.1 / 42.8 | 52.9 / 56.4 |
| NPAS | 34.4 / 40.1 | 27.3 / 41.3 | 33.0 / 42.7 | 37.7 / 52.2 |
| RIASEC | 22.5 / 31.3 | 27.5 / 36.7 | 26.1 / 35.1 | 49.7 / 53.1 |
| FTI | 40.7 / 45.0 | 42.2 / 47.5 | 41.6 / 52.8 | 55.7 / 59.7 |
| NIS | 27.0 / 35.7 | 34.3 / 36.5 | 25.3 / 40.4 | 52.2 / 56.4 |
| GSS 2022 | 47.7 / 50.3 | 47.3 / 52.3 | 45.6 / 52.0 | 55.7 / 59.2 |
| WVS 7 | 34.7 / 42.6 | 41.8 / 47.3 | 39.6 / 45.4 | 53.2 / 56.0 |

7. **Demographics are the input the model extracts the least from, and the one it is usually given.** With history it sits 3 to 9 points
   under the ceiling (NPAS is the outlier at 14). With demographics it sits 2 to 15 points under, and
   on all eight datasets the LLM given demographics scores *below* the supervised model given
   nothing (the per-item modal answer). Demographics do carry 1 to 5 points of information
   everywhere (ceiling column; 0.8 on NIS, 5.4 on RIASEC), so the problem is not that the information is absent; it is that the
   model turns a demographic profile into a stereotype that is wrong more often than the mode.
   Measured traits show the same gap (up to 15 points on NIS): the model under-reads numeric
   trait scores. Behaviour history is the one input it reads close to its information content.
8. **Without any subject information the LLM is below the per-item mode on every dataset**
   (by 2 to 9 points), consistent with run 1's finding that the uninformed model collapses onto one
   answer that is often not the modal one.

## Caveats

- One model (Gemini 3.5 Flash, thinking off), one draw per record, 1,000 subjects per dataset.
- openpsychometrics samples are self-selected online test takers; GSS and WVS are probability
  samples; SocSci210 participants are US panel respondents.
- The type vectors use the same three Big Five trait names on every dataset. Domain-matched traits
  (as the type-vector paper recommends) would likely raise the fitted-type numbers.
- GSS items have item-specific scales; W is computed per item on the normalised 0-100 range.
- SocSci210 has no raw-data ceiling (stimuli differ per study); its ceiling is 5-fold CV on the
  1,000 records and is conservative.
